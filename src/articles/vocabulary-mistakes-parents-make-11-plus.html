The 11+ Vocabulary Trap: Six Mistakes Parents Make

Most parents preparing a child for the 11+ know that vocabulary matters. What fewer realise is that a lot of the effort they put into it is wasted, and some of it actively works against the child.

The advice that circulates in forums and WhatsApp groups tends to reward visible effort: longer lists, more flashcards, more hours. Visible effort is not the same as progress.

Here are the mistakes we see most often, and what to do instead.

Mistake one: believing more words is always better

This is the big one. Somewhere along the line, parents picked up the idea that the child who knows the most words wins. Publishers and app makers are happy to feed that belief, because a list of several thousand words looks more impressive on a cover than a shorter, curated one.

The problem is that the 11+ does not test the whole dictionary. It tests a fairly stable core of words that appear again and again in comprehension passages, synonym questions and cloze exercises. Past that core, each extra word has a vanishingly small chance of turning up on the day, and the time spent memorising it is time not spent on something that will.

There is a second cost. Oversized lists bury the words that matter under hundreds that do not. A child working through a huge list cannot tell which words are worth real attention, so they spread themselves thin across all of them. The result is a shallow familiarity with many words and a confident grasp of very few.

A shorter list, built from words that genuinely recur in the exam, does the opposite. It lets a child go deep. That is why the 11+ Words app is built around a focused set rather than an exhaustive one. Quality over quantity is not a slogan here. It is the design.

Mistake two: treating a word as learnt once it has been seen

Flashcards are useful, but they encourage a particular illusion. A child flips a card, recognises the word, nods, and moves on. The parent ticks it off. Three weeks later the word appears in a passage and the child has no idea what it means.

Recognition is the weakest form of knowing. The exam demands more: picking the closest synonym from four plausible options, spotting the odd one out, choosing the word that fits a gap in a sentence. All of those require the child to retrieve the meaning actively, not just to recognise the shape of the word on a card.

The fix is to test rather than review. Ask the child to explain the word in their own terms, to use it in a sentence, to say what the opposite might be. If they cannot do that, the word is not learnt yet, however many times it has been seen.

Mistake three: learning words in isolation

A list of words with one-line definitions is the easiest format to produce and the hardest to retain. Words stick when they are attached to something: a sentence, a story, a situation, another word they are related to.

This matters especially for the 11+ because so many questions are about relationships between words. Synonyms, antonyms, words that belong to the same group. A child who has learnt “reluctant” as a standalone definition is in a weaker position than one who has met it alongside “hesitant” and “unwilling” and understands how they differ in shade.

Reading is still the best source of this kind of context, and no app replaces it. But when you are working from a list, insist on sentences. Make the child write one. Better still, make them write a slightly silly one, because the ones that make them laugh are the ones they remember.

Mistake four: cramming in the final months

Vocabulary is the one part of 11+ preparation that cannot be rushed. Verbal reasoning techniques can be picked up in weeks. Maths methods can be drilled. Words are different. They need repeated exposure spread over time, with gaps in between for forgetting and relearning.

Parents who start in earnest a few months before the exam often find that the child can recite words from a list but cannot use them under pressure. That is not a failure of effort. It is simply that the words have not had time to settle.

Start earlier than feels necessary, do less per session, and keep going. Ten minutes a day over a long period beats an hour on Sunday every time.

Mistake five: ignoring the words the child already half knows

Children arrive at 11+ preparation with a large stock of partially understood words. They have heard them, perhaps read them, and have a rough sense of what they mean. These are the cheapest words to secure, because the groundwork is already done. A single clear explanation and a couple of examples will often be enough to move them from vague to solid.

Yet most preparation ignores them in favour of entirely new words, which are far more expensive to learn. If you want quick wins, go through a list and ask the child which words they have met before but could not define. Deal with those first.

Mistake six: making it a chore

Sit a child down with a list of difficult words and a stern face, and you have taught them that vocabulary is punishment. They will do the minimum, forget most of it, and associate the whole subject with tension.

The children who do well with vocabulary are usually the ones who find words faintly interesting. That can be encouraged. Point out odd words in everyday life. Argue about whether a word is being used correctly. Let them catch you out. Play with the words rather than drilling them, and the drilling becomes almost unnecessary.

What this adds up to

Fewer words, learnt properly. Active recall rather than passive review. Context rather than isolated definitions. Time rather than intensity. Securing half-known words before chasing new ones. And enough enjoyment that the child does not need to be forced.

None of this requires more hours. Most of it requires fewer, spent better. If your current approach involves a very long list and a growing sense that it is not working, the list is probably the problem.
