11+ Vocabulary vs Verbal Reasoning: What's the Difference?

If you have started researching the 11+, you will have seen both “vocabulary” and “verbal reasoning” mentioned everywhere, often in the same sentence. It is easy to assume they are the same thing, or that one is simply a fancier name for the other. They are not, and understanding the difference can change how you plan your child's preparation.

This article explains what each one is, how they overlap, and how to split your time sensibly between them.

What is 11+ vocabulary?

Vocabulary is the knowledge of words and their meanings. It is a body of knowledge, much like times tables in maths. Your child either knows what “reluctant” means or they do not.

In practical terms, vocabulary covers:

  • Understanding what individual words mean
  • Knowing synonyms (words with similar meanings) and antonyms (opposites)
  • Recognising words in context, including words with more than one meaning
  • Spelling and using words correctly in their own writing

Vocabulary is also the foundation of the English paper in many areas, where comprehension passages assume your child can understand the language used. A child who meets a tricky word in a passage and has no idea what it means will struggle, however good their exam technique is.

What is verbal reasoning?

Verbal reasoning is a skill, not a body of knowledge. It tests how well your child can think with words, spot patterns, follow rules and solve puzzles that happen to be made of letters and language.

Typical verbal reasoning question types include:

  • Finding a word hidden across two others in a sentence
  • Completing word or letter sequences
  • Cracking simple codes, where letters are swapped for other letters or numbers
  • Choosing the word that does not belong in a group
  • Making a new word by changing or moving letters
  • Logic puzzles that use information given in short statements

Some of these questions need almost no vocabulary at all. A letter code puzzle, for example, can be solved by a child who understands the rule, regardless of how many fancy words they know. Others, such as finding the odd one out among a list of abstract words, lean heavily on word knowledge.

The exact mix of question types varies between exam providers and regions, so it is always worth checking what your child's target schools actually use.

The key difference in one sentence

Vocabulary is what your child knows. Verbal reasoning is what your child can do with it, along with a good deal of pattern spotting and logic that has nothing to do with word meanings.

Where the two overlap

The overlap is where many parents get confused, so it is worth being clear about it.

Strong vocabulary makes a large number of verbal reasoning questions easier. If a question asks which word is closest in meaning to “scarce”, a child who knows the word answers in seconds. A child who does not has to guess, and no amount of puzzle practice will fix that.

On the other hand, a child with a wide vocabulary can still stumble in verbal reasoning if they do not understand the question formats. They may know every word on the page but not realise how a particular puzzle works, or they may run out of time because they have never practised working quickly.

So the relationship works in both directions:

  • Vocabulary supports verbal reasoning by removing a major obstacle in many question types.
  • Verbal reasoning practice supports vocabulary by showing your child which words come up and how they are used.

Why the distinction matters for preparation

Because they are different things, they need different kinds of practice.

Vocabulary grows through exposure and repetition. Reading widely, talking about new words, and revisiting words little and often is far more effective than cramming a long list the week before the exam. Short, regular sessions work particularly well, because word knowledge sticks when it is encountered repeatedly over time.

Verbal reasoning improves through practice with question types. Your child needs to meet each format, learn the trick or rule behind it, and then build speed. This is where timed practice papers earn their place.

If you only do vocabulary work, your child may know lots of words but be thrown by unfamiliar puzzle formats. If you only do verbal reasoning papers, they may keep losing marks on questions that depend on word knowledge, and wonder why the practice is not paying off.

A sensible way to balance the two

Every child is different, but this approach suits most families.

  1. Start with a gentle check of where your child is. Have them try a few verbal reasoning questions and see where the marks are lost. Are they getting the logic wrong, or simply not knowing the words?
  2. Build vocabulary early and keep it going. Because it takes time to grow, vocabulary is best started well before the exam, with a little each day rather than long sessions.
  3. Introduce question types gradually. Learn one format at a time, understand the method, and only then add timing.
  4. Bring them together later. Once your child is comfortable with both, full practice papers show how they work together under realistic conditions.
  5. Review mistakes honestly. After each paper, sort the errors into two piles: “didn't know the word” and “didn't know how to approach the question”. That tells you exactly where to spend the next week.

A word of reassurance

It is natural to feel overwhelmed by how much the 11+ seems to involve. The good news is that vocabulary is one of the most improvable parts of preparation. Unlike some skills that feel fixed, word knowledge grows steadily with the right habits, and children often enjoy seeing their own progress.

You also do not need an enormous word list. More words are not automatically better. Beyond a sensible core of commonly tested vocabulary, the returns drop away quickly, and time is usually better spent understanding words properly and practising question types than racing through ever longer lists.

The short version

  • Vocabulary is knowing what words mean.
  • Verbal reasoning is using language and logic to solve particular types of puzzle.
  • Each supports the other, but they are built in different ways.
  • Little and often wins for vocabulary, while timed practice wins for verbal reasoning.
  • Work out which one is holding your child back, and focus there.

Treat them as two connected parts of the same journey, and your child will be far better placed on exam day.
