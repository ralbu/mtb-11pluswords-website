What Vocabulary Is Actually Tested in the 11+? (And What to Ignore)

If you've started looking into 11+ preparation, you've probably already downloaded three different word lists, each with hundreds of entries, and felt your stomach sink a little.

Where do you even start? Does your child need to know all of it?

The honest answer is no, and understanding why the 11+ tests vocabulary the way it does will save you a huge amount of wasted effort. This isn't a spelling bee. It's not about memorising obscure words for their own sake. It's about whether your child can understand and use words in context, quickly, under exam conditions.

Here's what's actually being tested, and what you can safely stop worrying about.

The Two Main Exam Styles — and Why They Test Vocabulary Differently

Most 11+ exams fall into one of two camps: GL Assessment or CEM-style tests (CEM itself has been phased out in some regions in favour of similar "tutor-resistant" formats, but the approach lives on).

GL Assessment tests vocabulary directly through a standalone Verbal Reasoning paper. Your child will meet question types like:

  • Synonyms and antonyms (choose the word that means the same, or the opposite)
  • Word relationships and analogies ("hot is to cold as light is to ___")
  • Hidden words, letter codes, and word completion puzzles

Because GL publishes official practice materials, it's the more predictable of the two, you can genuinely prepare for the format, not just the vocabulary.

CEM-style tests don't have a separate Verbal Reasoning paper. Instead, vocabulary is woven into comprehension and English tasks, mixed in with other question types without warning. There's no fixed list of question formats to drill, which is exactly why these exams are sometimes called "tutor-proof." The best preparation here isn't memorising question types, it's building genuine reading fluency and a wide working vocabulary that shows up naturally in unfamiliar contexts.

If you don't yet know which format your target school uses, it's worth checking before you commit to a prep style. A quick look at the school's admissions page or a call to the office will usually confirm it.

What "Vocabulary" Actually Means in This Context

This is the part most word lists get wrong. The 11+ isn't testing whether your child can recite a definition. It's testing whether they can:

  1. Recognise a word's meaning in context — spotting how a word is being used in a sentence or passage, even if they've never seen it written down before
  2. Identify synonyms and antonyms fast — under real time pressure, often 40–50 seconds per question
  3. Apply word relationships logically — understanding how two words relate to each other, not just what each one means individually
  4. Use precise vocabulary in their own writing — for the English/comprehension papers, being able to reach for a more accurate word than the first one that comes to mind

Notice what's missing from that list: spelling obscure words correctly, knowing etymology, or being able to define a word in isolation like a dictionary entry. Those things don't hurt, but they're not what's being scored.

What to Actually Focus On

Words that appear regularly in real exam papers. These tend to cluster around a fairly predictable "tier" of vocabulary, words that are a step above everyday speech but still commonly used in books, journalism, and formal writing. Think abundant, reluctant, articulate, benevolent, not obscure academic or scientific terms your child will never encounter again.

Reading widely, not just word lists. Children who read for pleasure regularly build vocabulary almost as a side effect, because they're seeing words used correctly in context over and over. A word list can accelerate this, but it can't fully replace it.

Synonym and antonym fluency. Since so many question types across both exam boards hinge on this skill, it's worth practising specifically, not just "does my child know this word" but "can they instantly produce a word that means the same or the opposite."

Applying words under time pressure. Knowing a word and retrieving it in 40 seconds are different skills. Timed practice matters as much as raw vocabulary size.

Why "Bigger List" Isn't the Same as "Better Prepared"

It's worth saying this plainly, because it goes against almost every word list you'll find online: once your child knows a solid, well-chosen core vocabulary, adding more words has rapidly diminishing returns.

A lot of sites and paid resources lean into the opposite message, because a bigger number feels more impressive and more sellable — "2,000 essential words!", "the ultimate 11+ dictionary!" It sounds thorough. But it usually isn't more useful, and it can actively work against your child for a few reasons:

  • Retention drops as volume rises. A child who's shown 2,000 words in the run-up to an exam will genuinely absorb and be able to use a fraction of them. A child who's shown 500 well-chosen, high-frequency words has a realistic shot at knowing most of them.
  • Exam word pools aren't that large. Question setters draw heavily from a fairly predictable band of vocabulary, words that are a step above everyday speech but still common in the kind of writing 10–11 year-olds actually read. Past a certain point, extra words on a list are increasingly rare or archaic ones that are unlikely to ever appear on a real paper.
  • Time is the real constraint, not word count. Every hour spent drilling one more word on an oversized list is an hour not spent on synonym/antonym fluency, reading, or timed practice — all of which matter more to the actual score.

So there is a threshold effect here, and it's a useful thing for parents to know about: past roughly the "high-frequency exam vocabulary" range, more words on a list rarely translates into better exam performance. The goal isn't to maximise the number of words your child has seen, it's to maximise the number they can reliably use under pressure. A tightly curated 500-word list a child actually knows will outperform a 2,000-word list they've only skimmed, every time.

What You Can Safely Ignore

  • Extremely rare or archaic words that show up in some word lists purely to pad them out — these almost never appear in real papers
  • Formal definitions and etymology — useful for genuine language-lovers, but not exam currency
  • "Bigger is better" word lists — treat any list running into the thousands with some scepticism; it's a sign of padding, not thoroughness
  • Trying to cover every word on every list you find — better to know 500 well-chosen, high-frequency words solidly than to skim 2,000 and retain none of them

A Simple Way to Think About It

If a word regularly shows up in books written for confident 10–11 year-old readers, or in the kind of newspaper articles parents read aloud at breakfast, it's fair game. If it only shows up in a specialist dictionary, it's very unlikely to be tested, no matter how impressive it sounds on a flashcard.
