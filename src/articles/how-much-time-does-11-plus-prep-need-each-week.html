How Much Time Does 11+ Prep Really Need Each Week?

If you have searched for an answer to this question, you have probably already met the panic-inducing version of it: forum posts describing children doing practice papers every single evening, tutors booked for multiple sessions a week, and timetables that look more like a revision plan for a professional exam than preparation for a ten or eleven year old.

It is easy to come away thinking that unless your family is committing huge chunks of every week to 11+ work, your child is falling behind.

The reassuring truth is that effective preparation does not need to dominate family life. What matters far more than the number of hours logged is how those sessions are structured, how consistently they happen, and whether your child is actually absorbing anything rather than just going through the motions. A shorter, well-planned routine will outperform a long, exhausting one most of the time.

Why “more hours” is the wrong question

Vocabulary, reasoning skills, and exam technique are built through repetition spread over time, not through marathon sessions. Cramming a large amount of practice into one sitting tends to produce diminishing returns quite quickly. A child's attention and retention both drop off once a session runs too long, so the extra time spent often adds very little beyond the point of fatigue.

There is also a motivation cost to consider. If 11+ preparation becomes synonymous with long, tiring sessions, children start to dread it, and that dread makes every future session harder to start and less productive once it begins. A sustainable weekly rhythm protects your child's relationship with learning, which matters just as much as the content itself, particularly if preparation needs to continue for several months.

A more useful way to think about weekly time

Rather than fixating on a target number of hours, it helps to think in terms of short, frequent, focused sessions rather than occasional long ones. Several brief sessions spread across the week, each targeting a specific skill such as vocabulary, comprehension, or a particular style of reasoning question, tend to be far more effective than a single lengthy session at the weekend.

A useful guideline is to treat weekday sessions as short and specific, and reserve a slightly longer session for the weekend when there is more time to work through a full practice section or past paper question without rushing. This keeps each individual session manageable while still covering the necessary ground across the week.

It is also worth building in genuinely free days where no formal preparation happens at all. Rest is not wasted time in this context. It allows new vocabulary and concepts to settle, and it keeps the whole process feeling sustainable rather than relentless.

What should actually fill that time

Time spent is only useful if it is spent well. A few principles help make sure shorter sessions still count:

  • Rotate focus areas rather than doing the same type of practice every day. Vocabulary one day, verbal reasoning the next, non-verbal or maths-style reasoning another day, keeps the brain engaged with variety rather than numbing repetition.
  • Prioritise active recall over passive review. Testing your child on words or concepts, rather than simply re-reading lists, builds much stronger retention for the time invested.
  • Fold vocabulary into everyday moments rather than treating it as a separate task. Words that come up naturally during a car journey, a meal, or while reading together tend to stick better than words memorised in isolation purely for test purposes.
  • Use real reading as a backbone. A short spell with a good book most days does more for vocabulary and comprehension over the long run than almost any dedicated worksheet, and it does not need to be counted as “exam prep” to count.

Adjusting as the exam approaches

The right weekly commitment will naturally shift over the course of preparation. In the earlier months, the priority is building foundations such as vocabulary and reading stamina, and shorter, lighter sessions are entirely appropriate. As the exam gets closer, it makes sense to introduce slightly longer sessions focused on timed practice and exam technique, since children need to get used to working accurately under time pressure before the real thing.

Even during this later, more intense phase, the same underlying principle holds. Quality and consistency still matter more than sheer volume, and protecting some rest days remains important for keeping stress manageable as the exam date nears.

Signs you have found the right balance

Every child is different, and the right weekly routine for one family may not suit another, so it helps to watch for practical signs rather than following a fixed formula borrowed from someone else's experience.

A good indicator is that your child can usually start a session without a battle and can still engage properly by the end of it. If sessions regularly end in tears, arguments, or your child switching off halfway through, that is a sign the current load needs scaling back, regardless of what any guide suggests.

Equally, if your child seems to be coasting through sessions with little effort and barely remembers what was covered a few days later, there may be room to add a touch more structure or variety, even if the total time stays similar.

Progress is also a useful marker. If vocabulary is genuinely expanding, comprehension is improving, and reasoning questions feel gradually less unfamiliar, the current routine is working, whatever the clock says about hours per week.

The bigger picture

There is no single correct number of weekly hours that applies to every child preparing for the 11+. What consistently works is a routine built from short, focused, varied sessions spread across most days, backed by genuine reading, with proper rest built in rather than squeezed out. This approach protects your child's energy and enthusiasm over what can be a long run-up to the exam, and in the end, a calm and sustainable routine tends to produce better results than an exhausting one ever could.
