Independent School vs Grammar School 11+: How Vocabulary Is Tested

If you are comparing grammar school and independent school entry, you have probably noticed that the two routes look quite different on paper. One often involves a local authority test, the other a school's own assessment or a shared online test. It is natural to wonder whether your child needs to learn different words for each.

The reassuring answer is that word knowledge matters for both routes, and the vocabulary your child builds is useful either way. What changes is how that vocabulary is tested. Understanding the difference helps you prepare calmly and avoid wasting effort.

Why vocabulary matters on both routes

Vocabulary sits underneath almost everything in the 11+. A child with a wide vocabulary reads comprehension passages more fluently, understands unfamiliar questions more quickly, and writes with more precision. That applies whether the exam is set by a grammar school consortium or by an independent school.

So the first point to take away is that you do not need two separate vocabulary plans. A steady, well-organised habit of learning and revisiting words serves both.

How grammar schools tend to test vocabulary

Most grammar school admissions in England use tests from GL Assessment. Individual schools or local authorities choose which subjects to include, so one area might test English, maths, verbal reasoning and non-verbal reasoning, while another uses only some of them. The older CEM style of paper has largely been replaced by GL in many areas, although it is always worth checking what your chosen school actually uses this year.

In these tests, vocabulary is often examined directly through verbal reasoning. Typical question types include:

  • Finding a word with the closest meaning to another
  • Finding a word with the opposite meaning
  • Choosing the odd ones out from a group of words
  • Working out which word has more than one meaning
  • Completing word analogies

These questions are short, timed and multiple choice. Your child has to recognise a meaning quickly, often with very little context. That rewards children who have met a word several times, in several settings, and can recall its meaning without having to think too hard.

Question styles also change over time. Newer formats have appeared in recent years, so practising a wide range of word tasks is wiser than rehearsing a single style.

How independent schools tend to test vocabulary

Independent school entry is more varied. Many senior schools use the ISEB Common Pre-Test, a set of online, adaptive, multiple-choice tests. Because it is adaptive, the questions adjust to your child's answers as they go. The tests include English and verbal reasoning sections, and ISEB states that pupils are assessed on their knowledge of vocabulary and definitions. The English section looks at areas such as reading comprehension, grammar, spelling and punctuation.

Other independent schools set their own papers, and some use different online providers. These often lean more on comprehension and written work, including creative writing. Here, vocabulary is tested less directly. A child is not asked for a synonym on its own, but is expected to understand a rich passage, explain what a word or phrase means in context, and use ambitious, accurate language in their own writing.

In other words, the grammar school route often checks whether a child knows the word, while many independent school papers also check whether a child can use it well.

So is the vocabulary itself different?

This is where it is easy to be misled. You may read that independent schools expect harder or more unusual words. In practice, there is no official word list for either route, and the evidence points to a difference in format rather than in the words themselves.

What does differ is the skill being rewarded:

  • Grammar school style tests reward quick recognition and recall of meanings, including synonyms, antonyms and multiple meanings.
  • Independent school style assessments reward understanding words in context and using them confidently in writing, as well as recognising them in reasoning questions.

A child who has secure, flexible knowledge of a good range of words is well placed for both.

What this means for your preparation

Because the underlying need is the same, a few habits will help whichever route you are considering.

Learn words in small, regular sessions. Short daily practice beats occasional long sessions. Revisiting the same words over several weeks helps them stick, which is exactly what quick-recall questions demand.

Learn more than one meaning. Many common words carry several meanings, and multiple meaning questions rely on this. Encourage your child to ask what else a word could mean.

Practise synonyms and antonyms together. Pairing a word with its near neighbours and its opposites builds the connections that grammar school style questions test.

Use words in sentences. Asking your child to say or write a sentence with a new word moves it from recognition to use. This is particularly valuable for independent school papers that include writing.

Read widely. Fiction, non-fiction, newspapers written for children and even good quality magazines all expose children to words in context. Reading remains the most reliable long-term vocabulary builder.

Talk about words. Mention interesting words at the dinner table or on the school run. Curiosity about language is a quiet advantage.

Check your own schools first

Formats vary between schools and can change from year to year. Before you settle on a plan, look at the admissions page of each school you are considering and confirm which tests are used, which subjects are included and whether the format is paper or online. If you are applying to a mix of grammar and independent schools, aim for broad vocabulary practice and add format-specific practice nearer the time.

The bottom line

The difference between independent school and grammar school 11+ vocabulary is mainly one of format, not of word lists. Grammar school tests tend to check recall of meanings directly, while independent school assessments often combine reasoning questions with comprehension and writing. Either way, a wide, flexible vocabulary built through regular practice and plenty of reading is one of the most useful things your child can bring to the exam room.
