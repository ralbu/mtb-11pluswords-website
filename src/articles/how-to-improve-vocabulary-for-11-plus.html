How to Improve Your Child's Vocabulary for the 11+ (Without Boring Them)

If you've ever sat down with your child and a vocabulary list, you'll know how quickly it can go wrong.

Eyes glaze over. Fidgeting starts. The same words that seemed manageable five minutes ago suddenly feel impossible. And then comes the guilt, yours for pushing, theirs for struggling.

The good news is that vocabulary doesn't have to be taught this way. In fact, the most effective methods for building a strong 11+ word bank have almost nothing in common with rote memorisation from a list.

This guide covers what actually works, practical low-friction strategies that fit into everyday family life without turning every evening into a battleground.

Why Traditional Vocab Lists Often Fail

There's nothing wrong with word lists as a reference. The problem is using them as the primary learning method.

When children sit down to memorise 20 words in one session, a few things happen:

  • They learn the words just well enough to pass a quick test, then forget them
  • They associate vocabulary practice with boredom and resistance
  • They learn definitions, but not how to use the word, which is what the 11+ actually tests

The 11+ doesn't just ask "what does tenacious mean?" It asks children to identify the closest synonym, spot a word used incorrectly in context, or choose the right word to complete a sentence. That requires a deeper, more flexible understanding of language.

The goal isn't to memorise a list. It's to internalise words so they become part of how your child thinks and communicates.

The Most Effective Strategies

1. Read, But Choose the Right Books

Reading is the single most powerful vocabulary builder, but not all reading is equal for 11+ preparation.

Modern children's fiction is wonderful, but it tends to use contemporary, accessible language. For the 11+, children benefit enormously from exposure to older or more literary writing, the kind where they'll regularly encounter words they don't know.

Good choices include:

  • Classic children's novels: Treasure Island, The Secret Garden, Around the World in Eighty Days, Little Women, What Katy Did
  • Roald Dahl: deceptively rich vocabulary wrapped in stories children love
  • C.S. Lewis: The Chronicles of Narnia uses sophisticated language naturally
  • Non-fiction on topics your child cares about: history, nature, science, sport

The key habit to build alongside reading is this: when your child encounters an unknown word, don't skip it. Stop, look it up together, and say it out loud in a sentence. This single habit, done consistently, builds vocabulary faster than any structured session.

2. The Word of the Day

This is one of the most effective low-effort strategies a family can adopt.

Each morning, introduce one new word, at breakfast, on the school run, or wherever works for you. Write it somewhere visible: a whiteboard, the fridge, a sticky note on the bathroom mirror.

The rule: anyone in the family who uses it correctly during the day earns a point, or simply gets acknowledged. It becomes a gentle game rather than a lesson.

Over a school year, this approach alone can introduce 150–180 new words, with almost zero formal study time.

Good sources for your daily word:

  • The word lists in the 11+ Words app
  • A page from a dictionary, opened at random
  • A word from whatever your child is currently reading

3. Synonyms Over Definitions

Here's one of the most underused strategies for 11+ preparation specifically: teach synonyms alongside every new word, not just meanings.

Instead of: "Tenacious means determined and persistent."

Try: "Tenacious means the same as determined, persistent, and resolute."

Verbal reasoning sections frequently present questions like: "Which word is closest in meaning to TENACIOUS?" with options including flexible, resolute, hasty, and cheerful. A child who knows the definition but not the synonym family will hesitate. A child who's heard tenacious and resolute together multiple times will answer instantly.

Make a habit of asking: "What's another word for that?" in everyday conversation. It's a 10-second exercise that compounds enormously over time.

4. Make It Competitive (If Your Child Responds to That)

Many children, particularly those who find formal study demotivating, respond brilliantly to competition.

Ideas that work:

  • A weekly quiz between siblings, or parent versus child
  • Apps and games that use quiz formats and track scores
  • A running family scoreboard for Word of the Day points
  • Setting a challenge: "Can you use five of this week's words before Friday?"

The content is the same. The framing makes all the difference.

5. Use the Words in Writing

Encourage your child to deliberately use new words in their school writing, stories, or even text messages.

The act of retrieving a word and using it correctly, rather than just recognising it on a list, moves it from passive vocabulary (words you understand when you read them) to active vocabulary (words you can use). The 11+ tests both, but active vocabulary tends to be more durable.

A simple prompt: "Can you use one of this week's words in your story tonight?" That's all it takes.

6. Short and Frequent Beats Long and Occasional

If there's one principle that underpins all of this, it's this: frequency matters more than duration.

A child who spends 10 minutes on vocabulary four times a week will outperform a child who does an hour-long session once a week. Memory research consistently shows that spaced repetition, encountering the same word across multiple sessions over time, is the most effective way to retain new information.

This is why daily habits (Word of the Day, reading, one synonym question) work better than Saturday morning study sessions. They're lower friction, they reinforce each other, and they build over time without burning anyone out.

What to Avoid

Avoid drilling too many words at once. Five words learned well is worth more than twenty words half-remembered.

Avoid making it a battle. If your child is resistant, reduce the pressure and increase the variety. Resistance is often a sign the method isn't working, not that your child can't do it.

Avoid skipping review. New words need to be revisited across several sessions before they stick. If you only encounter a word once, it rarely stays.

Avoid relying solely on apps or solely on reading. The combination of structured practice and natural exposure (through books and conversation) is more effective than either alone.

How Long Does This Take?

Most families begin 11+ preparation around 12–18 months before the exam. For vocabulary specifically, starting early has a compounding effect, words learned in Year 4 are reinforced naturally through reading and conversation by the time Year 6 arrives.

If you're starting later, don't panic. A focused 3–4 month programme, using the strategies above consistently, can make a significant difference. The 11+ vocabulary list isn't infinite, and a child who works through it systematically, with review built in, can cover the core words well within that window.

The Bottom Line

Improving your child's vocabulary for the 11+ doesn't require hours of formal study. It requires consistency, the right methods, and, most importantly, making it feel less like homework and more like something that's just woven into daily life.

Start small. Build the habit. The words will follow.
